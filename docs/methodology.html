Methodology

Every number on the board traces to a public government dataset. This page documents what each category measures, where it comes from, how it was computed and what to watch out for.

Geography

The units are the Department of City Planning's 2020 neighborhood tabulation areas (NTAs) — 262 areas built from census tracts. The board ranks the 197 residential NTAs (City Planning type 0) and excludes parks, cemeteries, airports, Rikers Island and eight special-purpose areas, which have few or no residents. NTAs are drawn so census survey figures hold up, not to match how New Yorkers define their neighborhoods, which is hotly disputed: when The New York Times asked readers to draw their own boundaries, many blocks were claimed by more than one neighborhood. Some familiar neighborhoods are split across two NTAs and others are merged into one, so read a rank as describing City Planning's area, not the neighborhood a resident has in mind. Boundaries come from the 2020 NTA file on NYC Open Data, which is clipped to the shoreline; land areas are computed from those boundaries (square feet ÷ 27,878,400).

Census categories

All demographic, economic, housing and commuting categories come from the American Community Survey (ACS) 2020-2024 five-year estimates, released by the Census Bureau on Jan. 29, 2026, as tabulated at the NTA level by the Department of City Planning and published as four Excel workbooks (demographic, social, economic, housing) on City Planning's ACS page. Values were read directly from those files with no adjustment. Dollar figures are in 2024 inflation-adjusted dollars; income means total money income from all sources before taxes, not just earnings. Each category's universe (such as "residents 25 and older") is stated under the board title; three worth spelling out:

  • Commuting shares divide by all workers 16 and older, including people who work from home. Mean commute time leaves out people who work from home.
  • Rent burden divides by renter households whose rent as a share of income can be computed. That leaves out households paying no cash rent and households with zero or negative income; it does not subtract them twice. In Greenpoint, for example, 6,453 burdened households ÷ (16,214 renter households − 419 not computed) = 40.9%. Median gross rent covers renter households paying cash rent.
  • Foreign-born means not a U.S. citizen at birth. Naturalized citizens count as foreign-born; people born abroad to American parents, or born in Puerto Rico or another U.S. territory, count as native-born.

Uncertainty

  • Margins of error. ACS figures are survey estimates. The board shows the Census Bureau's 90% margin of error (±) where City Planning publishes one. When two neighborhoods' intervals overlap, the data don't establish which is higher; that is not the same as showing they are equal, and overlapping 90% intervals are not a formal significance test (the Census Bureau's statistical testing guidance describes one).
  • Faded estimates. Three kinds are faded and labeled. Wide margin: a coefficient of variation above 30%, a cutoff this board chose because it is common in ACS practice; it is not a guarantee that estimates below it are reliable or that their ranks are stable. Estimated zero: when an estimate is zero, City Planning leaves the percent and its margin blank. The board shows those as 0% and ranks them, but a zero survey estimate doesn't prove no such residents exist; Hunts Point and Claremont Village, for instance, show an estimated 0% Asian share. No margin published: any other estimate without a margin. One value is missing entirely: Stuyvesant Town-Peter Cooper Village's median home value, since nearly every home there is rented.
  • Rank ranges. A neighborhood's page shows, beside each survey rank, a rough range of ranks: the best counts only the neighborhoods whose whole 90% interval sits above its own, the worst only those whose whole interval sits below. Estimates with no published margin are never counted as clearly above or below anything, and a median at its cap is treated as open-ended above. The range is a rough guide, not a confidence interval for the rank.
  • Derived categories. "Share never married" = never-married men plus never-married women, divided by population 15 and older, with a margin computed by the Census Bureau's formula for a derived proportion. "Single-family homes" = 1-unit detached share plus 1-unit attached share, with a margin from the root of the summed squared margins. Population density = ACS population ÷ land area; because land area is fixed, its margin is the population margin divided by the same area.
  • Event rates carry no margin on the board. Their counts are every record in the file, not a sample, but the population they're divided by is a survey estimate; for the median neighborhood its margin is about ±6%. That uncertainty isn't propagated into the rates, and the counts themselves miss unreported events and records without a usable location.
  • Capped medians. City Planning computes neighborhood medians from bracketed distributions whose top brackets are open-ended ($200,000-or-more income, $3,500-or-more rent, $2,000,000-or-more home value), so a median in the top bracket is reported at the cap: two neighborhoods for income, five for rent and three for home value. Values at the cap display with a plus sign and tie for first.

Rate options

  • Commuter-adjusted. Every per-1,000-residents rate (crime, crashes, 311, restaurants) can instead divide by a commuter-adjusted population, following the Census Bureau's formula: residents, plus jobs located in the neighborhood, minus jobs held by people who live there. Jobs come from the Census Bureau's Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics files (LODES 8, 2023, all jobs): the workplace file for jobs located here (4,585,129 citywide) and the residence file for jobs held by residents (3,769,974 for city residents), summed from census blocks through City Planning's tract equivalency. LODES counts jobs, not people, so a worker with two jobs counts twice on both sides; it leaves out most self-employment; and the New York residence file covers jobs within the state, so residents commuting to New Jersey or Connecticut aren't subtracted. Residents come from the 2020-2024 survey and jobs from 2023. Treat it as an alternative base for comparing job centers with residential areas, not a count of everyone present or a measure of anyone's risk: visitors, tourists and students aren't in it. On felonies, Midtown-Times Square goes from first per resident to 192nd of 197 on this base.
  • Each location once. For 311 categories, the board can count distinct recorded locations with at least one complaint instead of complaints. A location is a complaint's coordinates rounded to about a meter: a geocoded point, not a verified address, building or caller.
  • Small counts. Each event-based rate shows the count behind it. Rates built on fewer than 25 events are faded: one or two events either way would move them noticeably. They stay ranked, because dropping them would remove most of the low end of the eviction and rodent lists.

Make-your-own combinations

The "Mix your own" tab builds two kinds of user-defined rankings, computed in the browser from the same category values shown on the board:

  • Ratio. One category's value divided by another's, for each neighborhood. A neighborhood shows "no data" if either component is unpublished or the denominator is zero. Ratios involving a capped median (income, rent, home value) may be off in either direction, since capping the top of a fraction lowers it and capping the bottom raises it; rows that use a capped value are marked. Median rent ÷ median household income is shown as a year of rent (monthly rent × 12) as a percentage of income. It's a ratio of two medians, renters' rent over all households' income, not the typical renter's burden; the rent-burden categories measure that.
  • Blend. The average of a neighborhood's ranks across 2-6 chosen categories, weighted equally, where rank 1 in a category means the highest value, unless you switch that category to "lowest first." A neighborhood must have data in every chosen category to be ranked. Rank 1 is the lowest average.

Combinations are arithmetic on published figures, chosen by the reader; they are not editorial indexes, and margins of error are not carried through them.

Map, comparisons and similar neighborhoods

  • Map. The map shades each neighborhood by which fifth of the ranking it falls in; the key gives each fifth's value range. Parks, cemeteries, airports, Rikers Island and special-purpose areas are drawn dark and unranked.
  • Compare. The comparison table shows each survey value with its margin and fades uncertain estimates as the board does. On survey categories the leader among your picks is highlighted only when its interval clears every other pick's.
  • Most alike. Each neighborhood's page lists the five neighborhoods whose ranks are closest across all categories except population and land area: the average gap in rank position, as a share of the number ranked. It describes resemblance on these measures only and ignores margins of error.

Ranking rules

  • Ranks run 1-197 among neighborhoods with published data; ties share a rank.
  • Event rates are ranked on unrounded values and rounded only for display, so two neighborhoods can show the same rounded rate with different ranks. Survey figures are ranked as City Planning publishes them (to one decimal for percentages), so equal published values tie.
  • Bars are proportional to the value and always start at zero.
  • Borough filters and search change what is displayed but never change a neighborhood's rank, which is always computed against all 197.
  • No category is labeled "best" or "worst." High felony rates and high restaurant counts are both facts about a place; the reader decides what they mean.
  • Shared links carry the rate base and counting choices, including links to a neighborhood's profile or a comparison, so they reopen with the same numbers.

Limitations

  • Per-resident rates use where people live. Neighborhoods with many workers and visitors — Midtown, the Financial District — look extreme on anything divided by residents; the commuter-adjusted option changes that comparison but doesn't count visitors or tourists.
  • ACS five-year figures are period estimates pooled from surveys collected 2020-2024, not a count for any one year, so fast-changing neighborhoods lag reality. They are the newest census data for these areas. Newer annual population estimates exist for the city and the boroughs but not for NTAs; the Census Bureau's July 1, 2025 estimate puts the city at about 8.58 million, against 8.48 million in the five-year figures. Because one is a single-date estimate and the other a five-year pool, the gap isn't a measured growth rate. The 197 neighborhoods sum to 8,475,754; the small gap to the citywide five-year total is people living in excluded park, airport and institutional areas.
  • Excluded areas are the 65 non-residential NTAs: parks, cemeteries, airports, Rikers Island and eight special-purpose areas such as the Brooklyn Navy Yard.
  • 311 and crime complaints measure what gets reported, which varies with reporting habits, not only conditions.
  • The eviction, restaurant and tree files are administrative and inherit their agencies' lags and gaps.

Reproducibility

The pipeline — fetch script, source manifest, ACS extraction, point-in-polygon aggregation and the build step that produces rankings.json — is in the GitHub repository, with a requirements file for its Python dependencies. data/sources_manifest.json records each input's source, scope, download time, row count and SHA-256 checksum. The build asserts that record IDs are unique and fails if a source comes back short. Open Data files change as agencies revise records, so a fresh download won't exactly match; the raw files behind the current site are attached to the repository's data release. Dates: ACS workbooks downloaded Aug. 2, 2026; every other file Nov. 4, 2026. This project was built with the help of artificial intelligence (Claude); if a number looks wrong, check it against the source datasets linked above.
